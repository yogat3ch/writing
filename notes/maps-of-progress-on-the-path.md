We cannot solve our problems with the same thinking we used when we created them.
— [Albert Einstein](https://www.brainyquote.com/quotes/albert_einstein_121993?utm_source=share&utm_medium=select)

A deeper interrogation of the implications of this statement yields a question of how we might evaluate the "thinking used when we created them," in order to contrast it with new ways of thinking. This question implies that some kinds of thinking may be more appropriate to solve problems than others, or that a degree of metacognition and inner work are required to realize a form of thinking capable of solving the newer, gnarlier problems, or run the risk of attempting naive solutions that further exacerbate the problems. To address this issue, we must develop frameworks for understanding and evaluating inner phenomenological experience to discern what constitutes thinking that is more ontologically and epistemologically sound than our default modes of thinking. 

# Maps of Progress on the Path
Maps of spiritual terrain have been a nebulous yet sought after artifact to allow easier passage for people newer to the path. Maps come with benefits and drawbacks that any practitioner is wise to be aware of before engaging too seriously with a map. It's important to start from a premises of knowing that every map is situated in causes and conditions that gave rise to it's creation. Some maps may be appropriate for a particular juncture or tradition and inappropriate for another. Progress on the path is going to look different depending on a practitioners phase of life, practice style, practice lineage, predispositions, prior experience and present circumstances.
For example, if we come to practice through the door of suffering seeking to ameliorate it, then a reduction, or change in relationship to suffering, is going to indicate progress on the path. There are varying degrees of generalizability of the maps of the path and while most are not empirically validated there is substantial anecdotal validation from Dharma teachers over long periods of time for the traditional maps. 


### Intention
The intent that guides the search for a map can sometimes come from an intention to locate oneself amidst one's peers, sowing the seed for future obstacles to grow in one's path for reasons we'll explore. It  could also come from curiosity or a genuine sense of being uncomfortably lost. Reasons are many, as are maps. 

### Benefits of Maps
Maps can be especially helpful in a number of cases. We'll cover a handful of these cases to begin.

#### Teaching
Maps can be useful for experienced practitioners to corroborate with their lived experience to locate memories and experiences within the map. A teacher can then connect with their students experience and situate it in context of the map in order to provide them with appropriate next steps. 

#### Continuous Progress and Getting Unstuck
Most practitioners will recollect periods of practice that felt like a plateau. It's common for lay practitioners to encounter rough or demanding patches of life where practice loses emphasis and though it's might still be happening, there can be regression in cognition and conduct. Maps can be especially helpful to consult when in a plateau of practice to help point out a path forward and remind a practitioner that the terrain ahead is worth the effort. When one can orient to where they are, know what to focus on to move forward, and know, at least provisionally, when they've attained a stage, they can more consistently move forward with practice than if they're given overly simplistic instructions and left to their own devices.

#### Cross Tradition Comparisons
Maps of the practice also allow for interesting cross tradition conversations about the similarities and differences of various stages of awakening. These conversations can bridge traditions for people who need an exit from a style of practice for whatever reason but don't want to give up practice altogether, and they can add context and complementary practices to round out narrow practices or understandings that might be creating less than optimal outcomes for adherents. The informative exchange that has occurred as meditative traditions have spread out of Asia have created whole new ecosystems of practice derived from the synthesis of traditions, and have made their way into science labs and psychological frameworks producing adaptations suitable for wider audiences. 

### Limitations of Maps
When seeking out maps its important to acknowledge what George Box, a British Statistician first stated in 1976: "All models are wrong, but some are useful". To acknowledge the limitations of any map and avoid claiming de-facto universality of a given map will prevent many pitfalls. We've attempted to provide a map of maps here to give an impression of the longevity and diversity of maps of progress on the path of meditation and consciousness expansion. These maps don't all lead to the same place, but we hope that some of them will chart a terrain that's the right fit for you on your path to awakening. 

## Map Criticisms
Criticisms of maps often lead with the fact that the reality of developmental growth is rarely as elegant or linear as maps tend to suggest. Maps tend to oversimplify the messiness of real life development. We can hold this to be true but still find utility in maps. 

A map can also imply a hierarchical overlay onto reality, and onto people, to many people who encounter them. This may indicate that the map isn't appropriate for the reader, or that the reader has to reconcile with the realities of hierarchy. When referencing a map, there are inevitably people who are higher or further along than others, which has the potential to produce polarized relational dynamics between people identifying with different stages. This becomes a problem if the maps are used to hierarchize people rather than as a way to locate one's experiences and encourage one to approach appropriately scaled challenges conducive to growth. 

A map can also evoke analytical overthinking where one attempts to meticulously parse out what a map is pointing to rather than simply practicing. While intellectual dialogue about maps with a teacher familiar with the terrain can be fruitful, auto-didactic proliferation about maps or solo interpretation can create problems. 

Maps can also invoke competitive striving among peers. In attempts to progress faster along a given map, a practitioner is prone to misinterpretation or difficult experiences borne of frustration or going too deep too fast. 

Maps can also create the potential for "cheating," wherein reading about a stage on the path provides a practitioner the lexicon to describe the phenomenological state when under evaluation which makes it difficult for a teacher to know if they have a genuine realization of the stage or just a comprehensive reading of the literature about it. 

All of these potential pitfalls can ultimately hinder the cultivation of effortless single pointedness and awareness of awareness that opens the mind to subtler ways of knowing and often greater access to personal and universal insights, all of which don't necessarily require a map.

## Psychology Informed Maps
[The AQAL (All Quadrants / Levels / States / Lines / Types / Stages)](https://images.squarespace-cdn.com/content/51a0ef99e4b0673a4c034ab8/1373221911253-I9CKMOFPCM86G3TNULLF/Screen-Shot-2013-03-29-at-7.07.30-PM.png?content-type=image%2Fpng) - Claire Graves PhD & Ken Wilber
[The STAGES Model](https://integral-review.org/issues/vol-16-no-1-april-2020/) - Terri O'Fallon PhD
The STAGES model is a systematic, symmetric, model of ego development that has been empirically validated. It merges Ken Wilber's AQAL framework with Jane Loevinger and Susanne Cook-Greuters ego development model (MAPS) and it's testing paradigm. The STAGES Model extends the MAPS into the MetAware stages.  Progression proceeds via a recursive process defined by encounters with states, states becoming recognized and stabilized to become objects themselves, the stabilized state object(s) can then be viewed from various perspectives to form a stage. 
The test to establish a stage is based on sentence stem completions and evaluation asks three questions:
1. What level is the thinking indicative of: Concrete, Subtle, MetAware. 
2. Is it individual or collective? 
3. Is the relationship Passive, Active, Reciprocal, or Interpenetrative.
