## Typical Percentage Splits

For a thinning operation on a **50-acre plot of land in Georgia**, the typical arrangement relies heavily on how the timber is sold. Instead of a straight split of the mill's final payout, the industry standard involves ***"stumpage" pricing**, where a logger pays you a flat rate per ton of wood removed, or a **"shares" (percentage split) contract***.[](https://www.reddit.com/r/forestry/comments/1jfy9h8/selling_timber_on_the_shares_method_is_it_a_good/)


If you are using a standard percentage split (shares contract), the cuts typically break down as follows:

Typical Percentage Splits

- **Pulpwood & Low-Grade Thinnings:** **30% to 40% to the Landowner / 60% to 70% to the Logistics/Logging Company**. Because a first or second thinning mostly removes small, low-value pulpwood or "chip-and-saw" logs, the logging company takes a larger share. Their operational overhead (fuel, heavy machinery, and hauling to the mill) eats up a vast majority of the thin margin.[](https://permies.com/t/86729/Timber-harvest)


- **High-Value Sawtimber (Rare for early thinning):** **50/50 to 60/40 (favoring the landowner)**. If the thinning includes a decent amount of mature sawtimber, your split goes up because the product value outweighs the fixed logging costs.[](https://www.facebook.com/groups/BenningtonBlotter/posts/25228954500043067/)



Key Variables Impacting the Cuts on a 50-Acre Plot

A 50-acre tract is right on the line of what many Georgia logging operations consider a profitable standalone trip. Because moving heavy feller-bunchers and skidders is highly expensive, several localized factors will shift the percentage cut:[](https://www.facebook.com/groups/819949195289830/posts/1396497827634961/)


| Factor                   | Impact on Landowner Split          | Notes                                                                    |
| ------------------------ | ---------------------------------- | ------------------------------------------------------------------------ |
| **Distance to the Mill** | 📉 **Lowers your cut** if far away | Hauling logistics are highly sensitive to fuel prices.                   |
| **Tract Accessibility**  | 📈 **Raises your cut** if easy     | Good highway access and solid, dry ground mean fewer delays.             |
| **Thinned Volume**       | 📈 **Raises your cut** if heavy    | Low-volume thinnings increase the logger's risk, driving up their share. |
| **Site Prep Needed**     | 📉 **Lowers your cut**             | Building log landings or skid roads reduces your payout.                 |

Crucial Steps to Protect Your Revenue

1. **Hire a Consulting Forester:** Forestry consultants typically charge **5% to 10% of the sale value**, but they usually make you far more money. They will mark the exact trees to thin, market the tract to multiple local buyers to create a bidding war, and ensure the loggers follow Georgia’s **Best Management Practices (BMPs)** so your land isn't rutted or destroyed.[](https://www.haydenoutdoors.com/timber-value-per-acre-worth/)

   
2. **Beware of "Diameter-Limit" Cuts:** Never sign a shares contract that simply allows a logger to "cut everything above X inches." This is high-grading (taking the best, leaving the junk) rather than a proper silvicultural thin that sets your remaining stand up for a massive future payoff.[](https://sunshinesaved.com/2024/12/23/the-economics-of-timber-sales-for-a-family-woodlot/)

3. **Draft a Legal Contract:** Ensure you have a written contract detailing liability, exact payment schedules tied to mill scale tickets, and a formal **shutdown clause** for wet weather.[](https://www.youtube.com/shorts/rC2bp26y_tY)

## Expected Values Based on Your Wood Composition

Having a mix of **70% planted loblolly pine** and **30% old-growth loblolly pine** changes your strategy completely. You are no longer looking at a low-value, uniform pulpwood harvest.

Because of the high-value logs mixed in, **do not accept a standard 60/40 or 70/30 logging split**. Instead, your property calls for a **"pay-as-cut" stumpage structure or a sealed lump-sum bid**, broken down by the specific wood classes you have on site.

Expected Values Based on Your Wood Composition

Instead of a generic percentage of total mill value, Georgia loggers will quote you a flat price per ton (stumpage) for different products. The split of revenue shifts heavily in your favor as the tree size increases:[](https://www.reddit.com/r/forestry/comments/1awif8z/40_year_old_loblolly_pine_in_heard_county_ga/)


-
- **Planted Loblolly (The 70%):** If this is a first or second thinning, it will yield **Pulpwood** and **Chip-and-Saw** (mid-sized logs).
  - *The Cut:* Loggers take **60% to 70%** of the mill value because processing and hauling thousands of small trees is labor-intensive. You will receive a lower price per ton for this portion.
- **Old-Growth Loblolly (The 30%):** These are massive, mature trees yielding high-grade **Sawtimber** or potentially premium **Plylog/Pole** timber.
  - *The Cut:* **You should keep 60% to 65% of the mill value** for these trees, while the company takes 35% to 40% to cover logging/hauling. Your stumpage price per ton for old growth will be significantly higher than the planted pine.

The Strategy: Safeguarding Your Old Growth

Old-growth loblolly is incredibly valuable, but it is also highly vulnerable during a thinning operation. You must establish strict rules before heavy machinery enters your plot:

1. **Mark the Old Growth Separately:** A thinning operation's main goal is to remove weak or overcrowded trees to let the best ones grow. For your planted section, the logger will likely remove every 4th or 5th row and thin inside the remaining rows. However, the old-growth section should be treated with selective thinning. Do not let a logger clear out your high-value old-growth sawtimber just to make their trip more profitable.[](https://www.aces.edu/blog/topics/forestry/thinning-loblolly-pine-stands-to-benefit-wildlife-timber-production/)


2. **Protect the Residual Stand (BMPs):** Massive skidders and feller-bunchers can easily skin the bark off your remaining old-growth pines or compact the soil around their roots, which introduces disease or kills the trees. Your contract must include strict penalty clauses for excessive damage to the trees left standing.
3. **Use the [Georgia Forestry Commission (GFC)](https://gatrees.org/learn-explore/landowner-resources/):** Before talking to a timber buyer, utilize the [GFC Consulting Forester Directory](https://gfcarcserver.gfc.state.ga.us/portal/apps/dashboards/df3dded758da45c0851432c3f6e9cca7) to find a local, registered forester. They will cruise your 50 acres, estimate the total tonnage of pulpwood vs. sawtimber, and manage a competitive bidding process.[](https://gfcarcserver.gfc.state.ga.us/portal/apps/dashboards/df3dded758da45c0851432c3f6e9cca7)

## Step 1: Set Up a 1/20-Acre Sampling Plan

I’m sorry to hear that the local foresters haven’t been supportive. It is incredibly frustrating when you are trying to protect your investment and can't get clear guidance.

You can absolutely **cruise your own timber** to estimate its value. Because timber is sold by the **ton** in Georgia, your goal is to find the **volume/weight** of the trees.[](https://timbermart-south.com/resources/state-stumpage-prices/georgia/)


Step 1: Set Up a 1/20-Acre Sampling Plan

You do not need to measure all 50 acres. Instead, you will use **circular sample plots** to get an accurate representation of the tract. A **1/20-acre plot** has a radius of exactly **26 feet, 4 inches**.[](https://extension.umn.edu/natural-resources/forestry-and-wildlife/forestry/managing-woodlands/sampling-and-measuring-timber)

For a 50-acre plot, a standard sampling intensity is **28 total plots**.[](https://extension.umn.edu/natural-resources/forestry-and-wildlife/forestry/managing-woodlands/sampling-and-measuring-timber)


-
- Allocate **20 plots** randomly across the 70% planted loblolly section.
- Allocate **8 plots** across the 30% old-growth section.
-

When you walk to a sample point, stick a flag in the ground. Use your measuring tape to pull out 26'4" in every direction to form your circle. **You will only measure the trees inside this circle.**[](https://extension.umn.edu/natural-resources/forestry-and-wildlife/forestry/managing-woodlands/sampling-and-measuring-timber)



Step 2: Measure Diameter (DBH)

**DBH** stands for Diameter at Breast Height, measured at exactly **4.5 feet above the ground** on the uphill side of the tree.[](https://content.ces.ncsu.edu/timber-sales-a-planning-guide-for-landowners)


1. Wrap a standard tape measure horizontally around the tree at 4.5 feet.[](https://extension.psu.edu/valuing-standing-timber)



2. Record the **circumference** in inches.

3. Divide the circumference by **3.14 (Pi)** to get the DBH. *(Example: A 44-inch circumference ÷ 3.14 = 14-inch DBH).*[](https://extension.psu.edu/valuing-standing-timber)



4. Tally your trees by their product class:

   - **Pulpwood:** 6 to 9 inches DBH
   - **Chip-and-Saw:** 10 to 13 inches DBH
   - **Sawtimber (Your Old Growth):** 14+ inches DBH[](https://extension.msstate.edu/publications/pine-timber-volume-weight-conversions)


Step 3: Measure Height Using an Altimeter

To find a tree's height using a standard geographic/barometric altimeter, you will use basic geometry. You must stand exactly **100 feet away** from the base of the tree.

1. Stand 100 feet back. Look at your altimeter and record the base baseline reading (e.g., you are at 500 feet elevation).
2. Look through your sight line to the **merchantable top** of the pine tree (where the tree narrows to about 4 inches in diameter, or where it forks). Note the altitude reading on your altimeter.[](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/233541623_Equations_for_Predicting_Green_Weight_of_Loblolly_Pine_Trees_in_the_South)


3. **Subtract the base reading from the top reading.** Because you are exactly 100 feet away, every 1 foot of elevation change read on your instrument equals 1 foot of vertical tree height. *(Example: Top reading 585 ft – Base reading 500 ft = 85 feet of merchantable height).*

## How to Make the Fieldwork Fast

***Yes, you should sample all 28 plots.***

On a 50-acre tract, there are **1,000 total potential 1/20-acre plots** available on the landscape. Measuring **28 plots** means you are sampling exactly **2.8% of your total land** (1.4 acres out of 50).

While it sounds like a lot of fieldwork, stopping short will compromise your leverage against a logging company for two critical reasons:

- **Planted Pine Variability:** Even though planted sections look uniform, factors like soil quality, previous disease, or how aggressively the 2016 thinning was done create massive volume differences across 35 acres. You need 20 plots to smooth out those natural spikes and drops.
- **The High Value of Old Growth:** Your 15 acres of old-growth contains the highest-paying sawtimber. Because older trees grow much more irregularly than planted rows, sampling fewer than 8 plots could cause you to drastically underestimate your most valuable asset—costing you thousands of dollars at the negotiation table.

How to Make the Fieldwork Fast

You can easily knock out 28 plots over a single weekend if you split the labor:

- **Person 1 (The Centerpoint):** Stands at the center flag holding the end of the measuring tape. They act as the anchor and record the data on a notepad.
- **Person 2 (The Cruiser):** Pulls the tape out 26'4" to establish the boundary, wraps the trees to find DBH, and uses the altimeter from 100 feet away.
